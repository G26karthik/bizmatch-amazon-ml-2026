01 · THE PROBLEM

Which records describe the same business?

Source 1 is a clean reference list of businesses (name, address, country). Source 2 and Source 3 hold copies of those businesses written with typos, abbreviations, reordered addresses, different scripts and missing fields, mixed with decoys: records built to look like a real business but belonging to none.

For every Source 1 business we must list the IDs of its copies. A business can have zero copies, and the metric rewards an empty answer in that case. Training data covers the US and India; the test set also includes France, with no labels at all.

Our key observation: every Source 2/3 record belongs to at most one business (true for all 7.64 million training pairs). So instead of comparing businesses to records, we ask each record a single question, "which business are you a copy of, if any?", and group the answers.

03 · METHOD

From twelve million records to one decision per business

Each step narrows the problem for the next. Steps marked runs live are executed on the demo slice in section 04 with exactly the code from our submission.

Normalise

Transliterate Indian scripts, expand abbreviations, split house numbers, build consonant skeletons.

Block

Three TF-IDF searches per country (name + address, address, name). Only plausible pairs survive.

Features

46 signals per pair: fuzzy name and address similarity, house numbers, ranks, competition between records.

LightGBM cascade

A small model prunes weak pairs, a larger one scores the rest. Each record picks its best business.

Bi-encoder

Fine-tuned multilingual-e5 adds candidates the text searches miss (recall 98.9%).

7 cross-encoders

Transformer models re-read the uncertain pairs; a stacker combines every score.

Expected-F0.5 decision

For each business, choose the set of records that maximises the expected metric.

France + calibration

Label-free rules from the data generator, test-like recalibration with two LoRA-tuned 7B judges.

Try the normaliser

Calls ber.normalize on the server. Different spellings of one business collapse to the same skeleton, which is what lets blocking find them.

Try:

Validation we could trust

  • Split by business, never by pair. A business and all its records go to validation when hash(id) % 10 == 0.
  • Two halves. Half A chooses every setting; half B is only used to report. If A and B agree, we are not fooling ourselves.
  • Look like the test set. The test set has more unmatched records (40% vs 26%), so we hide 19% of businesses during training. Their records stay, but become unmatched.
  • It worked: US and India scored 0.9913 on the test set, the same as validation half B.

Reading the numbers

  • Retrieval recall: share of true copies that blocking put in the candidate list.
  • Oracle F0.5: the best score possible if the model were perfect on those candidates.
  • Half A / half B: settings are chosen on A; B is untouched, so A ≈ B means no overfitting.
  • Empty answer: the score of predicting nothing for everyone, a sanity baseline.
05 · THE METRIC

How F0.5 is calculated

The score is computed per business and then averaged over all businesses (macro average). F0.5 weighs precision more than recall, so a wrong match hurts more than a missed one.

F0.5 = 1.25·TP / (1.25·TP + FP + 0.25·FN)
  • TP: predicted records that really are copies of this business.
  • FP: predicted records that are not (wrong matches).
  • FN: true copies we did not predict (misses).
  • A business with no copies scores 1 for an empty prediction and 0 for anything else.
  • It is the usual Fβ = (1+β²)·P·R / (β²·P + R) with β = 0.5, rewritten in counts.
  • Final score = mean of the per-business F0.5 over every Source 1 business.

The final submission at a glance

  • 5,866,818 matches over 1,732,544 test businesses: 3.39 per business, against 3.46 true copies per business in training.
  • 5.7% of businesses left empty, against 5.6% of training businesses with no copies.
  • US and India on test: 0.9913, equal to validation half B. France rose from 0.953 to about 0.979 with the label-free rules.
  • Every match lies inside the 9,365,329 candidate pairs, and both files pass the official validator.

What we learned

Understanding how the data was made paid off more than any single model. After the core model was in place, the largest gains came from questions about the data itself: how many unmatched records the test set holds, what those records look like, and how the generator writes a French copy. A validation set we could trust let us answer those questions without spending leaderboard uploads.
